    One view of GPU metrics across two sites, with Thanos

    The use case

    Nordhaven has a permanent site in Paris, and a site in Finland that only exists while its H200s are rented. Nordhaven wants one place to query every GPU of both sites, and the Finland metrics have to stay queryable after the Finland site is deleted. Here Finland writes to Thanos Receive in Paris, Paris keeps its own Prometheus with a sidecar, and one Thanos Query reads both and the bucket. The design and its alternatives (Mimir, VictoriaMetrics) are in ADR 0009.

    What ran

    The Paris side ran on 21 October: Receive, the sidecar, deduplication, uploads, and downsampling on simulated blocks. A CPU-only test of the Finland site on the same day carried its metrics to Paris over the internet. On 22 October the Finland site ran with two H200s for about half an hour. Their DCGM series reached Paris, first on whole GPUs and then with one H200 split by MIG. They were still queryable in Paris after the Finland site was deleted.

  fprom -- "remote write, mutual TLS<br/>client CN = tenant" --> recv
  pprom -- "2 h blocks" --> bucket
  recv -- "2 h blocks" --> bucket
  comp <--> bucket
  sg --> bucket
  q --> pprom
  q --> recv
  q --> sg
  graf --> q
    • Finland pushes, Paris never reaches in. The Finland firewall opens nothing to Paris, and Paris can't query a site that's been deleted. Port 30291 on the Paris CPU worker is open only to the Finland node addresses. Tested from my laptop, it doesn't answer.
    • The certificate names the tenant. Receive takes the tenant from the client certificate's common name, so the Finland site can only write as fin. The CA is generated in the cluster by a Job and never goes into Git.
    • Paris doesn't depend on the CPU worker. The Paris Prometheus stays on the control plane and uploads through a sidecar. It keeps working, alerting included, while the worker that runs Receive, Query and the compactor is powered off.

    Downsampling and the last block (simulated data)

    The 1 hour level needs 10 days and doesn't appear. The last line is real data, not simulated: a 12 second fin-sim block that Receive flushed and uploaded when the CPU worker was powered off. That is the behaviour the Finland site depends on. When Receive stops, it closes its head and uploads what it holds (cmd/thanos/receive.go at v0.42.4), so the last minutes of a site reach the bucket without waiting for a 2 hour block.

    Finland over the internet, without a GPU (21 October)

    Before renting H200s, I tested the Finland write path with a Finland control plane on a CPU instance (CPU.8V.32G, FIN-01). The first attempt never got a machine: at 12:16 UTC the provider put the instance in status "error". The retry ran from 15:33 to 15:40 UTC (while it ran, after deletion):

    Check Result
    First site="fin" series in the Paris Query 15:39:11 UTC, the same second kube-prometheus-stack was Healthy in Finland
    Tenant tenant_id="fin", from the client certificate installed at 15:37 UTC
    Finland remote write 45,032 samples sent, 0 failed, 0 retried
    After make fin-down (15:40:26 UTC) 16 minutes later, the Paris Query still serves the 11 Finland up series, last sample 15:39:53 UTC

    That covered what only a real Finland site could test: port 30291 open to its actual address, the certificate reaching it, and remote write across the internet.

    It also taught me something about querying a deleted site. An instant query only sees a series for 5 minutes after its last sample. My first query asked for "now", 16 minutes after the site was gone, and found nothing, although Receive held all of it. Queries about a deleted site have to look back, as MODE=after now does (last_over_time(...[3h])), and a dashboard showing it needs a time range that covers the run.

    Reading the labels on the Finland Prometheus itself (labels) explains the 51:

    • Each MIG instance gets its own 20 series, labelled GPU_I_ID and GPU_I_PROFILE="MIG 3g.71gb", and dcgm-exporter attributes each one to the tenant pod using it (team-a/mig-a on instance 2, team-b/mig-b on instance 1).
    • The split GPU keeps 11 series for the device itself, mostly DCGM_EXP_GPU_HEALTH_STATUS. So 11 + 20 + 20 = 51.
    • GPU1, whole, has 40, and 41 over the run: one more series appeared while it was busy, as on the L4.

    Two things on the dashboard are wrong after a MIG split, and neither is fixed yet:

    • DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL isn't reported for MIG instances. Every query that counts GPUs with it, including the "GPUs per site" figure above, sees 1 H200 where there are 2.
    • The recording rule groups by the GPU's UUID, which both MIG instances share with their parent GPU. It shows GPU0 as one 51, not two instances of 20.

    The fix is the next change to the rules and the dashboard: group by GPU_I_ID as well, and count GPUs from a field every GPU and MIG instance reports.

    The dashboard filtered on Finland at 10:23 UTC, six minutes after the MIG split. The table shows GPU0's 51 series as one row.

    Series per GPU, and what they mean for capacity

    From the L4 run in Paris on 21 October (capture, follow-up) and the H200 run in Finland:

    GPU DCGM series
    L4, idle 40
    L4, under the training job 41 (DCGM_EXP_CLOCK_EVENTS_TOTAL{clock_event="power_cap"} appeared)
    H200, whole 40, then 41 under load
    H200 split into two MIG instances 51 (11 for the device, 20 per instance)

    Two things make the count move, both visible in the labels:

    • One series per clock-event reason. gpu_idle was there from the start. power_cap appeared when the training job hit the L4's power limit.
    • Pod labels on a busy GPU. dcgm-exporter adds exported_pod, exported_namespace and exported_container to the series of a GPU a pod is using. Every new job puts its own pod name on new series, so the count churns with the workload, not only with the number of GPUs.

    So the per-GPU figure for capacity planning is the maximum over a busy period, not an idle reading. The recording rule is evaluated every minute and kept with the rest of the data, so max_over_time(gpu:dcgm_series:count[...]) gives it.

    At 40 to 51 series per GPU, 1,000 GPUs make 40,000 to 51,000 DCGM series and 10,000 GPUs make 400,000 to 510,000, before pod-label churn. That is arithmetic from three GPUs, not a load test. For scale, the DCGM series were 91 of the 66,491 series in the Finland Prometheus: almost all of the rest came from the control plane's API server and kubelets.

    What's simulated

    Both are listed in what is simulated and labelled on every series:

    • site="fin-sim": a second Prometheus in Paris posing as the Finland site, writing through the same mutual TLS path.
    • site="sim-backfill": the synthetic blocks for the downsampling check.

    The H200 series, the L4 series and the Finland write path are real.

    What two sites and three GPUs can't show

    • A Receive hashring or replication. There is one Receive and one site writing to it, so the write path has a single point of failure. The Finland Prometheus keeps its WAL and retries through a short outage.
    • Cardinality at scale. The figures above for 1,000 and 10,000 GPUs are extrapolated, not load-tested.
    • Downsampling of real data, until the Paris uploads span two days.
    • The Finland site's last block in the bucket. The Finland data sits in Receive's head. It reaches the bucket when Receive cuts a block or stops, as the 12 second fin-sim block above shows. I haven't listed the bucket since the H200 run.

    Reproduce it

    make thanos-check DRY_RUN=server     # without a GPU: flags, renders, dry run in Paris
make thanos-test-rules               # promtool tests of the GPU recording rules
make thanos-check-mtls               # local end to end of the Finland write path
make thanos-capture MODE=hub         # Paris: stores, dedup, uploads, port closed
make thanos-backfill-sim             # SIMULATED: five days of blocks, then
make thanos-capture MODE=bucket      # about an hour later: the 5 minute blocks
make thanos-capture MODE=fin         # while the Finland site runs
make thanos-capture MODE=after       # after make fin-down
